# Why Urdu Text Looks Different in Every App — A Simple Guide to Nastaliq, Fonts and Calligraphy

If you read Urdu on your phone, you have probably noticed something strange. The same sentence can look elegant in one app, cramped in another, and completely different in a screenshot someone sends you. Nothing about the words changed. What changed is the *style* of the script — and for Urdu, style is not decoration. It is the reading experience itself.

## Two styles, one language

Arabic is usually written in Naskh, a style that sits neatly on a straight horizontal line. Urdu, however, is traditionally written in Nastaliq — a flowing style where words slope diagonally, letters stack on top of each other, and a single line needs much more vertical space.

Nastaliq grew out of Persian calligraphy traditions and became the defining look of Urdu poetry, books and newspapers. When an app shows Urdu in a plain Naskh-style font, native readers can still read it — but it feels like reading English in a typewriter font. Correct, but foreign.

## Why your phone keeps "breaking" Urdu

Most phones, apps and websites are built Latin-first. Their default fonts, line heights and text boxes assume letters that sit in a row. Nastaliq breaks all three assumptions:

- Lines need almost double the height, so text gets clipped.
- Long words stack upward instead of stretching sideways.
- If the device has no Nastaliq font at all, it quietly substitutes another style.

This is why a design that looks perfect in English can fall apart the moment real Urdu text is pasted in — and why choosing proper [Urdu fonts](https://www.urdu-nigaar.com/) matters more than most designers expect.

## Calligraphy never really left

Nastaliq itself began as handwriting. For centuries, calligraphers shaped everything from Quran manuscripts to shop signs, and that heritage is still visible today. Religious phrases are the clearest example: the Bismillah that opens chapters of the Quran is written in dozens of decorative forms, from classical scripts to modern digital styles. Anyone who has tried to copy it into a design app knows the problem — type it, and the beauty disappears; what you need is the ready-made calligraphic form. Collections of [Arabic calligraphy](https://www.urdu-nigaar.com/blog/bismillah-arabic-text-copy-paste/) versions of the Bismillah, in both spelled-out text and single-character ligature form, exist precisely for this: designers, students and teachers who need the authentic shape, not an approximation.

## What this means for everyday readers and creators

You do not need to be a calligrapher to benefit from this. Three habits help:

1. **Read in a real Nastaliq font** when you can — long poetry and articles become noticeably easier.
2. **Give Urdu text room** in designs: taller line spacing, fewer words per line.
3. **Copy text, not pictures of text** — screenshots cannot be searched, translated or resized; proper Unicode text can.

Urdu is one of the world's great literary languages, with a script that carries its own art form inside it. The apps are slowly catching up. Until they do, understanding the difference between Naskh and Nastaliq — and choosing fonts that respect it — is the simplest way to make Urdu on screen look the way it does on the page.
